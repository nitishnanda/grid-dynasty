# Apex Ladder

An F1 career simulator. Start in karting at 7 (or join British F4 at 16 with the real 2026 grids) and climb F4, F3, F2 and F1, with lateral moves into IndyCar, WEC, Formula E, Super Formula, GT and more.

## Run it

No install, no build. Open `index.html` in any browser, or serve the folder:

    python3 -m http.server 8000   # then visit http://localhost:8000

Keep `index.html`, `f1-data.js` and `f1-engine.js` in the same folder. Your career saves in the browser's local storage.

## What's in it

- 5 talent tiers (Prospect, Talented, Late bloomer, Wonderkid, Generational) that shape your career curve
- Probabilistic promotion, retirements, academy deals and a driver market
- Two mid-season paddock briefings (card draws) per year with real career effects
- Evolving F1/IndyCar/WEC/Formula E car cycles with regulation resets
- Real 2026 lineups where available (F1, F2, F3, British F4, IndyCar, WEC, Formula E, Super Formula and more)

## Install on your phone

Open the Pages link in Safari (iPhone) or Chrome (Android), then Share, Add to Home Screen. It runs fullscreen like an app and works offline.

## Gameplay notes

- Pick a talent tier at the start (or leave it random) to test different career paths
- Choose Push, Balance or Coast before each race: push is faster but riskier (worse if your racecraft is low), coast is steadier with fewer DNFs
- F1 weekends include sprints. FP1 only appears as an academy outing or an F1 stand-in
- Qualifying and races show time gaps (to the leader, or the margin over P2 if you win). Gaps are scaled per series from real-world spreads and vary by weekend, wet weather, safety cars and the regulation era
- Karting weekends are a heat plus a final, over 10-11 rounds (the OK and OK-Junior seasons follow the WSK / European / World calendar)
- Family funds pay for seats in the junior series. If they run out, only a strong record keeps sponsors around
- Red Bull juniors debut at Racing Bulls. A mid-season promotion to Red Bull is rare, a demotion is common
- Pick or randomise family wealth (poor, middle class, rich, old money). Poor families can run out of money in karting unless you keep finishing top five
- Contracts: sign for 1-4 years and press Continue at the end of a season instead of re-deciding every year
- F1 rookies need time. Titles in your first two F1 seasons are rare (a generational talent can still do it in year two, like Hamilton in 2008)

## Calibration (F1)

F1 runs on a lap-time model fitted to the F1DB open database (every result, qualifying lap and retirement 2010-2026):

- Driver ratings for the 2026 grid come from a driver + team-season model on 6,890 qualifying laps (1 rating point is about 0.035 s a lap). The 2026 grid is then tuned to the 2026 season so far (rounds 1-17): each driver's rating reflects current form from teammate qualifying gaps, race results and reliability, and the sim reproduces real wins, podiums and points per driver (RMS error about 13 points a season over 100 simulated seasons). Domination is kept realistic on purpose: a driver who has won more than about half the races so far loses a little pace (rivals' upgrades, risk), the effect is weaker in a genuinely dominant era, and pole streaks fade too. Across simulated seasons the top driver wins 14+ of 24 about 11% of the time, 17+ about 2% and 18 poles essentially never, while a generational driver in a clearly faster car with an ordinary team-mate wins about 11 (a dominant car about 13) with 18-20 podiums. The very best drivers' season-to-season form swings are smaller than the rest of the field's (F1DB), which also stops a star in the best car from winning only 3-4 races. Incidents and safety cars cluster into chaos weekends (real F1 retirements run about 25% higher in safety-car races), passing a car that is barely slower is hard while passing a backmarker is easy, and winners start from the front row more often. Every circuit has its own character (Monaco is processional and wall-heavy, Baku and Jeddah bring safety cars, Monza and Spa reward power, Suzuka and Barcelona reward aero, Singapore and Budapest reward mechanical grip), and each car concept has its own strengths for the whole regulation cycle, so a team is quick at some tracks and slow at others. Race day now includes tyre strategy: teams read the weekend's tyre wear with some error (worse for weaker teams) and pick one or two stops, a wrong call costs time, and a safety car at the right moment is a free stop for some drivers and a wasted one for others (your result row shows your stops and compounds). One-stop races are about 40% of the field, like 2026. A driver's good or bad weekend is shared between qualifying and the race, which keeps front-runners' grid positions meaningful; back-of-grid starters retire more often, as in real F1. Races also have virtual safety cars (about 31% of grands prix), red flags (about 9%, mostly on chaos weekends: gaps reset, free tyres, standing restart) and first-lap pile-ups that take out two or three cars from the same part of the grid. Teams can win places through the pit window (undercut or overcut), very high tyre wear brings three-stop races, and car concepts differ in how kind they are to their tyres. Racecraft counts for half in a 19-lap sprint. Sprint-day form is its own draw, and the front-runners get cleaner first laps while the pack reshuffles more. Each 2026 driver also carries fitted current-form offsets: a one-lap offset and spread (so Antonelli and Verstappen swing between pole and bad Saturdays while Ferrari sits steadily P3-P6), a race-pace offset and a fragility factor. These fade by half each year after 2026. Over 3,000 simulated seasons the 2026 grid reproduces every driver's real qualifying average within about 0.2 places and their points within about 4 a season. Cars also develop through the season: a team gains about 0.3% of a lap per 1% it starts behind (plus or minus 0.4% either way, fitted to OpenF1 2023-26), and 2026 uses each team's real drift. Rookies carry a small experience penalty (about 2.0 / 0.8 points in seasons one and two), eased after Antonelli showed a second-year driver can lead the title fight. Talent tiers sit on the same scale as the real grid: a Generational driver peaks around Verstappen's level from about 22-23 and holds it to about 34-35 (top three in the standings about 80% of prime seasons, the title about 70% of the time in the fastest car), a Wonderkid peaks around Leclerc and Norris and holds it to about 32, and everyone slides faster after 35. The best AI drivers also start their decline later (a 92+ driver holds on to about 34). Top drivers have far fewer off weekends and scrappy Saturdays, so they rarely miss Q3 in a good car (about 1% in a top-two car, about 6% for elite drivers across the real 2023-26 grid). Seasons have shapes for the top drivers: steady, a slow start then locked in (Hamilton 2017-18, Vettel 2013) or a flying start that the chasers eat into. The season screen shows your car's current pace rank and gap, and a Car pace table shows the whole order and how it has moved since round 1, so a mid-season upgrade race can flip it (Red Bull and McLaren in 2024). The report card judges every row against the car: meeting the car is a B, a front-row qualifying average is top marks whatever the car, racecraft allows for the places a car normally gains from its grid slot, consistency is compared with what is normal for that end of the field, and finishing where the car belongs counts as extracting it whatever the team-mate did. Missing a race through injury is rare (about 4% of F1 seasons). Every injury says what caused it (a practice crash, appendicitis, a cycling accident...), including career-ending ones. Seat losses follow F1DB: about 4.5 drivers leave the grid a season including retirements, and drivers clearly above the grid average are very rarely dropped. A Generational or Wonderkid F2 champion is nearly always given an F1 seat (a team clears out a weaker driver). The contract desk lists higher categories first. In-season development is bounded by what happened in 2023-26 (first quarter against last quarter of each season): the fastest car dropped two places once (Red Bull 2024) and never three, and only one top-four car left the top four (Aston Martin 2023). In the sim the fastest car loses two places in about 8% of seasons and never three, a top-four car leaves the top four in about 3%, and the midfield closes up but rarely breaks into the top four. Season debriefs and report cards judge you against the car's place in the order across the whole season, so a car that slides back mid-season is allowed for (and a third-fastest car finishing sixth is par). Now and then (about 5% of seasons, only when the fastest car's margin is small) the fastest car is also the most fragile, like McLaren 2012: it fails far more often than the rest and usually loses the title. Between rule resets the chasing pack sits further behind the top two cars, so about 31% of seasons have three teams each winning 3+ races (real 2010-25: 31%) and about 87% have two (real: 81%). Mid-season title fights with three teams within 45 points of the lead are about 1 season in 11 (real 2010-25 is about 1 in 8). Every season line in the career file shows the car's average place in the pecking order (its rank after each round, averaged) with its best and worst rank, so you can see whether you had the fastest car or had to extract it, and the career summary gives the average over your F1 years. F1 entry now follows the FIA Super Licence: 40 points over the three seasons before the F1 season (F2 top three 40, F2 P4 30, F3 champion 30, FRECA champion 25, IndyCar champion 40, and so on), and age 18. A team can petition for a force majeure exception at 30-39 points (rarely granted: Herta's 32 points were refused in 2022) or for the 2024 age dispensation for a 17-year-old with 40 points. Friday practice needs 25 points. FP1 outings are rookies-only (no more than 2 F1 starts) and need 25 points or a full F2 season; each weekend with 100 km of clean running adds 1 licence point, up to 10. A driver who has left F1 gets no FP1 outings, and stand-in calls go to reserve drivers: a driver dropped from F1 is often offered another team's reserve role (a Red Bull driver typically goes to Racing Bulls) alongside a seat in another series. Red Bull's second seat is a revolving door: a Racing Bulls driver who beats their team-mate or finishes in the points gets the Red Bull call, Racing Bulls itself turns drivers over after about two years (real: about two), and a driver who has not earned the promotion after three years is not renewed. Red Bull's number 1 priority (the "Verstappen curse", from 2027): when Red Bull's lead driver is generational, or a wonderkid who has won a title and been there two years or more, the team builds around him and the other car is slower (about 0.26 s a lap for a generational lead, less for a wonderkid champion), the second driver makes more mistakes under the pressure, and a second driver who is far behind by round 12 can be replaced by the best Racing Bulls driver mid-season (the player's second seat also gets a much bigger "dropped by team" card). The second driver takes over as lead by beating him in the championship: very likely for a generational talent, rare for anyone else. Under the curse the number 2 scores about 0.55 of the lead's points (Pérez 2021-24: 0.35-0.67). The report card and debrief judge a number 2 against the car, not the team-mate. Racing Bulls is never quicker than Red Bull on pace and never wins a championship (192 simulated seasons: no titles, 0% of the time faster). Racing Bulls drivers last about 2.2 years with about 30% lasting a single year. A constructors' championship table sits next to the drivers' standings, in the debrief and on each season line. When a team petitions the FIA to speed up your licence (force majeure at 30-39 points, or an age dispensation at 17), the contract desk shows a note saying who asked, whether it was granted or refused and the odds. TEST SWITCH: while testing, every generational talent is signed by the Red Bull Junior Team as soon as they reach the single-seater ladder (`TEST.genRedBull` in f1-data.js; set it to false to go back to the random academy draw). Academies each have their own edge: winter-series points and how often they send their juniors (Red Bull 13 always, Ferrari 9, Mercedes 7, McLaren 6...), FP1 slot weight (two F1 teams means more slots), skip-a-rung odds and petition odds. Karting-end drivers who are generational or wonderkids reach F4 a year early, a dominant young F4 champion can skip FRECA straight to F3, and a proven senior-series winner (a title or podium in IndyCar, Super Formula, Formula E, WEC...) can get a discretionary licence. Drivers from poor families get an underdog work-ethic bonus (+1.6 rating, +0.3 for middle-class), whatever their talent. F1 and the junior single-seaters have stewards: penalty points for causing collisions, unsafe releases and the like (about 2 a season in Balance, 3.7 in Push), and 12 in a rolling year is a one-race ban. Every GP row has a one-line race story. A rival is built from the drivers you keep finishing next to, and shows in the debrief, the career file and two wheel cards. F1 contract talks let you ask above the going rate (greedy asks can make the team walk away), choose the length, and ask for a Number 1 clause (Red Bull only) or a win bonus. The career file ends with a "life after racing" epilogue (team principal, broadcaster, mentor, businessman...). Injuries have real recovery times (a broken foot or leg is 3-4 rounds, about 6-8 weeks) and never hit a driver who is out of the seat. The winter break is 3-4 months, so a late-season injury does not cost rounds next year; if recovery runs into the first weeks of winter you start the season slow instead (rehab, not calibrated to the new car: up to about -3 pace fading over a few rounds, and about 1.6x worse for Red Bull's second driver). A rare winter injury can still cost rounds if it lasts long enough to run into the season. Points ties are settled the FIA way (most wins, then second places, and so on) and the debrief says how. Controversies: a close championship can come with a decision fans never forgive (a race director error, track limits not penalised, a team let off for cheating, a car under the weight limit not excluded). The result never changes, but the debrief says so and the career file counts it ("5 titles on paper but fans say 6 after Abu Dhabi 2046"); a narrow win can carry an asterisk. Red Bull's mid-season demotion only happens from round 8, when you are clearly behind your team-mate on points (under 60%), and a first season is protected. A released Red Bull driver is offered Racing Bulls and the best Racing Bulls driver moves up. The contract desk and driver file show your points, the three-season window and what is missing; a promotion that the FIA blocks says why. Red Bull's junior programme is the fast track: its juniors run winter series (FRME) for extra licence points, have TPC mileage banked, get twice the FP1 slots (two F1 teams), a much better chance of an age or points petition, and a wider skip route (a dominant FRECA champion can skip F3; an F3 champion or runner-up can go straight to F1). Other academies get a smaller version. The career file lists every injury with its cause, puts the career-ending one in the summary, splits F1 grands prix from sprints (as the record books do), and gives a brutally honest projected place on the all-time F1 list. The career file ends with a written summary of the whole career and a table by category (karting as one, then each series) with races, wins, podiums, poles, DNFs, points and the average season report-card rating.
- Car pace, track-by-track fit, knockout track evolution, one-lap noise and its heavy tail, race-day form, grid-position value, safety cars, grid penalties, and mechanical vs incident DNFs (by regulation age and grid slot) are all set from the data.
- The AI driver market (rookies, drops, retirements, transfers, debut ages, career lengths) is tuned to F1DB seat-change statistics.
- Race pace is fitted to lap-by-lap timing from OpenF1 (2023-2026) and Jolpica (2022). The race-pace spread behind the leaders (about 1.9% of a lap for the P10 car in a reset year, about 1.3% after that), teammate race-pace gaps, how much race day reshuffles the qualifying order, safety-car rates and timing, slow stops and time penalties all come from that data. One-lap gaps converge as the rules mature. A dominant car also gets a small one-lap edge, so it locks out the front row more often.
- Retiring in the last 10% of a race still gets you classified, laps down, as in real F1.
- The 2026 start is fitted team by team to the real 2026 season so far (17 rounds): each team's strength, its race pace against its qualifying pace, and its reliability. Mean qualifying position, mean finish and points per race match real 2026 for all eleven teams (for example Racing Bulls score points in about 62% of starts and Alpine in about 49%, as in real life). The race-pace edge fades by half each winter and a rule reset wipes it.
- Wet races: about 7.5% of grands prix are properly wet (OpenF1 2023-26: 6% start on inters, 18% see any rain at all).
- F1 team moves (F1DB 2010-25): about 10% of drivers at the top three teams change team a year, 26% at teams ranked 4-6 and 31% lower down. Reigning champions almost never move.
- After F1, offers depend on age: young leavers go to Formula E and WEC, early thirties to IndyCar and DTM, veterans to WEC and IMSA, almost nobody to NASCAR or Supercars (54 drivers matched to Wikipedia series standings).
- Retirement rates per series come from the Ret/DNF cells of the Wikipedia results grids: about 12% of starts in F4/FRECA/F3, 17% in F2, 14% in DTM, 15% in Formula E, 7% in Supercars.
- Junior seat costs follow published estimates: F4 about 100-250k euros, FRECA 350k-1M, F3 about $1.2M, F2 about $3M.
- F1 pay is fitted to reported base salaries of the 2026 grid (2014-2026, in GBP). It rises with F1 experience, last season's championship position, the team and titles; rookies sign for about half.
- The junior ladder follows Wikipedia standings for GP2/F2 (2005-2025), GP3/F3, Formula Renault Eurocup/FRECA and Italian and British F4. That covers the chance of a step up by final position (an F2 champion reaches F1 the next year about 60% of the time, P2-3 about 40%, P6-10 about 5%), the rookie learning year, and how dominant champions are. F2 and F3 champions cannot defend their title. Every season debrief ends with a report card: season in context (result against what the car should deliver), car extraction (against the team-mate, adjusted for the rating gap), one-lap pace, racecraft, consistency, progress through the year and clean driving, each with a grade and a one-line explanation. When a step up does not come, the contract desk now says why (the grid is already locked, teams wanted someone younger, the seats went to academy drivers). An F2 champion or podium finisher without an F1 seat is offered a reserve contract with an F1 team while racing elsewhere, which puts them first in line for next winter's vacancies. F4 graduates step up to FRECA first (about half of F4 champions go straight to F3), as most real F4 drivers do. The step-up odds are tuned so simulated careers reproduce the real next-season rates for each step.
- Ageing follows the F1DB curve for everyone, including the player: flat to 28, then slower each year from 29.
- The senior series (IndyCar, NASCAR, Formula E, Supercars, DTM, WEC, IMSA) are fitted to Wikipedia season standings 2008-2025: how often the champion repeats (IndyCar ~25%, NASCAR almost never, Supercars ~40%), the champion's share of wins, how many drivers win in a season, and how tight the top of the table is. Every winter each of those series reshuffles its line-ups: about 20% new faces and about 30% of drivers changing team, skewed towards the faster cars (Wikipedia entry lists 2008-2025). Series you are not racing in used to freeze their line-ups, which is why the same names kept winning titles. Past champions stay in their seats longer and can win again after a gap, as real stars do (about 40% of IndyCar and NASCAR champions had won before).
- Validation targets the sim is scored against include pole-to-win rate (52-56%), front-row and top-3 win rates, mean finish by grid slot, race gaps, qualifying gaps by position, teammate gaps, DNF rates, the champion's car rank, wins by car rank and the title-margin split.

Data: F1DB (https://github.com/f1db/f1db), CC BY 4.0; lap timing, stints and race control from OpenF1 (https://openf1.org) and the Jolpica F1 API (https://api.jolpi.ca).
